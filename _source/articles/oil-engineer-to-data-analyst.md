Before data, I was an **Asset Production Engineer at NNPC**, working across oil production facilities in Nigeria. It was demanding, technical work, and I was good at it.

Then I relocated to the UK to join my wife. Overnight, the experience I'd built didn't open the doors I'd hoped it would. Like many people who move countries, I had to start again.

[[fig:career-journey]]

## Starting from the bottom

My first jobs in the UK had nothing to do with engineering. I spent a week working in a fish processing factory. After that, I spent five months installing solar panels.

I'm not embarrassed by those jobs. They paid the bills, and they taught me something important: **nobody was going to hand me my next career. I'd have to build it.**

So in the evenings, I started teaching myself data skills.

## Why data?

Looking back, the move made sense. As an engineer, I'd always worked with numbers: production figures, performance trends, operational reports. What I hadn't had were the modern tools to turn that data into clear answers quickly.

Data analytics drew on everything I already knew, the problem-solving, the attention to detail and the business sense, and added a skill set UK employers were actively hiring for.

## The three tools I focused on

I didn't try to learn everything. I focused on the three tools that kept appearing in UK data analyst job adverts:

1. **Excel:** still everywhere in business, and the foundation for everything else.
2. **SQL:** the language for getting data out of databases, which almost every analyst role asks for.
3. **Power BI:** the tool for turning data into reports and dashboards that people actually use.

Within six months of arriving in the UK, I landed my first role as a Data Analyst.

## What actually worked

These are the lessons I'd pass on to anyone making the same switch.

### Focus beats breadth

It's tempting to collect courses in Python, R, Tableau, machine learning and more. Employers hiring junior analysts mostly want to see that you can **get data, clean it, analyse it and present it clearly**. Excel, SQL and Power BI cover that. Go deep on those first.

### Your past experience counts more than you think

Career-switchers often believe they're starting from zero. You aren't. Whatever you did before taught you how a business works, how to solve problems and how to deal with people. **Frame your previous career as domain knowledge**, because that's exactly what it is.

### Learn by building, not just watching

Tutorials feel productive, but real learning happens when you take a messy dataset and try to answer a real question with it. Projects also give you something concrete to talk about in interviews.

### Consistency beats intensity

Fitting study around full-time work is hard. Steady, regular practice, even an hour in the evening, adds up far faster than occasional marathon sessions.

### Show your work

Employers can't see your skills unless you show them. A small portfolio of dashboards and analyses, with a short explanation of the business question behind each one, makes a real difference.

## Where it led

Today I'm a **Senior Data Analyst**, a Microsoft Certified Professional and a member of BCS, The Chartered Institute for IT. I also founded **Intelligence With Data**, where we help businesses automate their reporting and help people build the same skills I had to piece together on my own.

## If you're where I was

If you're working a job that doesn't use your abilities, studying in the evenings and wondering whether it's possible: **it is.** The path isn't always quick or comfortable, but it's real.

That's why I run a free live **Power BI and SQL class every Friday**. It's the practical, hands-on session I wish I'd had when I started.
